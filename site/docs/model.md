# Model assumptions and limits

The model calculates geographic positions, illustrative blast radii, cloud height, and overlap intensity. It is calibrated to the read-only prototype's visual behavior and is not a measured effects forecast. Fallout, audible, and visible-range models remain future capabilities.

`src/domain/geodesy.ts` uses a spherical Earth with mean radius **6371 km**, matching the reference prototype. Great-circle distance uses the haversine formula. Destination calculations use a spherical bearing formula. Longitudes are normalized to [−180°, 180°), so a route crossing the antimeridian remains short. Inputs with non-finite coordinates, latitude outside [−90°, 90°], negative distance, or travel beyond the antipodal surface distance are rejected.

These formulas are appropriate for a map-scale illustrative simulator. They do not include terrain elevation, Earth ellipsoid corrections, or local surveying precision. Effect models consume geographic distance in kilometers and document their own assumptions here.

## Device input

The fixed catalog preserves the prototype's 47 displayed choices. Internal yields are megatons of TNT equivalent. Conventional entries are labeled separately from nuclear entries; the planned fallout view must exclude conventional-only strikes. MIRV entries store both displayed aggregate yield and explicit per-warhead yield. Each delivered impact uses the latter value. The three modeled loadouts are 3 × 0.175 Mt, 10 × 0.12 Mt, and 10 × 0.5 Mt.

Custom input accepts finite megatons from 0.000000001 Mt, the prototype's minimum, through 1,000,000,000 Mt, the catalog's 1 Pt upper study. These bounds keep model calculations within a defined range. Group separators must divide three digits consistently; malformed text cannot resolve to a device profile and must not launch.

## Scenario framing

The camera estimates the prototype's light-effect radius as 4.8 km times the cube root of yield divided by 0.01 Mt, bounded to less than the antipodal surface distance. Its framing horizon is 1.08 times that radius for a single device or three times for a MIRV, subject to the same surface cap. The renderer converts this distance to a zoom using viewport dimensions and latitude. This estimate positions the camera; it is not a forecast or a rendered blast model. On a narrow MIRV click, the map may reframe the deterministic spread once if fewer than two impact centers remain visible outside the controls. It changes only the camera, not modeled targets or radii.

## Incoming missile and MIRV separation

The incoming path is a visual reconstruction of `prototype-code/app/missile-flight.mjs`, not a ballistic trajectory or travel-time prediction. At launch, the camera supplies a viewport-edge start and one bent control point; all three are stored as geographic coordinates. On each frame the renderer projects those points through the current camera, then samples their quadratic screen-space curve with progress raised to 1.16 over 6.5 seconds. The camera adapter selects the nearest world copy at the antimeridian. Smoke emission points stay on this projected route; their decorative CSS-pixel drift is damped over 0.22 seconds and fades within 3 seconds. Adjacent puffs use their own ages and seeds. None of these screen-space optical choices changes physical distance. Single-launch entry angles vary by a deterministic golden-angle sequence, beginning at the upper right for a reproducible reference frame; successive clicks in a multi session retain the first group's general bearing.

For a MIRV click, `src/domain/mirv.ts` follows the prototype's deterministic spread. With `n` warheads and one warhead's light-damage radius `r` in kilometers, the spread scale is `min(0.12πR, max(1.7r, 8 km))`, where `R = 6371 km`. Target `i` lies at radius `spread × (0.28 + 0.72 × sqrt((i + 0.42)/n))` and bearing `18° + 137.507764° × i` from the clicked aim point. This golden-angle placement is a visual pattern, not a dispersion or targeting prediction. Each target retains its own geographic coordinate and receives the catalog's per-warhead yield.

The shared carrier lasts 6.5 seconds and branches after about 60% of that flight. Before separation only one projectile and trail appear; after separation, each reentry vehicle follows a projected cubic branch beginning at the carrier position and matching its screen-space tangent. Its second control point lies 18% of the split-to-target displacement before the target, following the prototype path so the vehicle continues moving into impact instead of stopping there. Warheads arrive 45 ms apart in normal motion, or 8 ms apart on the shortened flight clock. The path is reprojected as the camera moves. Continuous screen motion is a visual contract; it does not estimate real missile speed, atmospheric entry, or delivery accuracy.

## Blast radius and expansion

`src/domain/blast.ts` reproduces the prototype's `radiiForYield` screening scale for one impact. For yield `Y` in Mt and the 10 kt reference `Y₀ = 0.01 Mt`, light, moderate, and severe radii are respectively `4.8`, `1.6`, and `0.8 km × (Y/Y₀)^(1/3)`. The fireball radius is `0.14 km × (Y/Y₀)^0.4`, bounded by the severe radius. All zones are nested and capped at the half-circumference of the 6371 km spherical Earth; the same prototype floor of 0.001 km prevents a zero-size effect. At 21 kt, the light radius is about 6.15 km, matching the rounded label in `Nuke Blast Top.png`.

These constants are transcribed from `prototype-code/app/page.tsx` and calibrated to its imagery, not derived from a validated damage calculation. Yield scaling omits burst height, terrain, shielding, building type, weather, and uncertainty. The conventional catalog entries use the same visual scale for now; their inclusion does not imply nuclear fallout. The field is a visual screening estimate and must not be used as emergency guidance.

The application stores radii with the strike, so subsequent device changes cannot resize an existing blast. A 6.5-second carrier flight leads into a 4.6-second expansion; the flash lasts 1.1 seconds. The field uses `1 − (1 − t)^2.15` for fast-first growth, with `t` normalized to the expansion duration. The renderer unprojects each visible mask sample and measures great-circle distance from ground zero in kilometers, so the surface radius remains meaningful across zoom, latitude, longitude wrapping, and camera pitch. The distance grid is cached between animation frames and rebuilt after camera or viewport changes. The mask is capped to roughly 640 × 420 samples and smoothed on display. Color anchors, the shock ring, and the brighter white-gold corona are visual reconstructions, not physical wave-front or radiation predictions.

## Pitched ground presentation

The same geodesic distance grid drives both damage color and the warm center glow when the map is pitched. Each displayed sample is unprojected through the current MapLibre camera to a surface coordinate before evaluating distance. Boundary rings, ruler points, and markers are projected from spherical destinations. Thus the field and inspection graphics use one geographic ground plane even when perspective changes their screen shape. At zero pitch the original radial Canvas glow is retained for visual continuity. The glow's kilometer radii are converted from the screen-light size at ground zero; they are optical presentation choices, not additional physical effects. Mask resolution can soften a steep pitched edge by a few CSS pixels. Terrain elevation, occlusion by buildings, and atmospheric scattering are not modeled. The raised fireball is a separate layer described below.

## Raised fireball

`prototype-code/app/fireball-renderer.mjs` supplies the visual timing and procedural flame reference. The dome's horizontal radius and height each equal the strike's modeled `fireball` radius in kilometers. Its radius grows with the same fast-first blast-front fraction as the ground field and reaches full size when that front reaches the fireball boundary; visibility rises over the first 14% of that local growth. The surface cools from white-yellow to mottled gold/orange by 42% of the total blast expansion. These are optical curves, not measured temperature or pressure histories.

The GPU mesh is a hemisphere in local Web Mercator coordinates. Its center is the geographic strike target, its size is converted from kilometers to local Mercator units at the target latitude, and its world copy follows the camera across the antimeridian. Equal horizontal and vertical Mercator scale approximates a local hemisphere; curvature, terrain elevation, building occlusion, atmospheric scattering, and physically calibrated luminance are omitted. Very large hypothetical yields therefore remain illustrative. A separate 2D field and flash remain visible if WebGL fails. The texture stops at the completed blast age for deterministic settled rendering.

## Blast inspection

`src/domain/blastZones.ts` divides each blast into fireball, severe, moderate, and light annuli. For a surface radius `r` on the same 6371 km sphere, the enclosed area is `4πR² sin²(r/(2R))` square kilometers; each zone area subtracts its inner cap from its outer cap. This stable form agrees with the prototype's spherical-cap calculation even for small radii. The 21 kt light annulus is about 105.5 km², as displayed in `MarkersAnd Popups.jpeg`. Labels, colors, and damage descriptions reproduce the prototype's illustrative language. Neither a zone boundary nor its area predicts actual casualties or building damage: shielding, terrain, burst height, and local construction are not modeled. Marker positions and the ruler are geodesic destinations from ground zero; the current map camera only projects them for display.

## Cloud-profile instrument

`src/domain/cloud.ts` follows the display-height formula in `prototype-code/app/page.tsx` (`cloudModel`), with `Nuke Blast with Fireball.png` as the visual calibration for the default 21 kt Fat Man. Inputs are **one impact's yield in megatons**, an illustrative burst height in kilometers, and whether the device is nuclear. For a nuclear impact, the atmospheric term is `burstHeight + 7.5 + 8.4 × log10(1 + yield / 0.01)` km. For a conventional impact it is `burstHeight + 0.12 × cbrt(yield / 0.000011)` km. The final displayed peak is the greater of that term and burst height plus the modeled fireball radius, capped at 1,000,000 km so the instrument stays finite. The default Fat Man's 0.503 km burst-height setting gives a 12.1 km rounded peak. Conventional MOAB/FOAB use 0.003 km; the prototype supplies fixed heights for Davy Crockett, Little Boy, Fat Man, and the three MIRV systems, while other nuclear devices use a bounded cube-root setting. MIRV heights use each reentry vehicle's yield, not the aggregate catalog yield.

During the 6.5-second carrier flight the cloud remains at standby. During the 4.6-second blast expansion, progress `t` goes from 0 to 1 and the height fraction rises as `1 − (1 − t)^2.45`. The selected device sets the standby scale; once launched, the monitor follows the latest group's first ignited warhead and its frozen device and yield even if selection changes. Reset removes the launch and returns the readout to standby. The silhouette is a separate, deterministic Canvas illustration whose puffs, glow, and phase labels follow the same progress; its visual shape is not part of the numerical model. Heights above 120 km receive a “luminous plume” and space-line treatment only as a visual cue for hypothetical scale studies.

These equations are transcribed from the prototype for visual parity, **not** derived from atmospheric observations or a validated cloud-rise model. They omit atmospheric stability, winds, precipitation, terrain, burst-medium coupling, and time-varying buoyancy. The extreme-yield cap and space-line marker do not predict an actual plume in space. The height is an illustrative screening display, not a measured altitude, fallout boundary, weather forecast, or emergency-planning estimate.

## Multi-strike overlap

`src/domain/overlap.ts` follows the visual-response equation in `prototype-code/app/range-field.mjs` and the field construction in `prototype-code/app/page.tsx`. Each impact retains its own yield, geographic target, and blast radii. `blastDamageIntensity` maps great-circle distance in kilometers to a normalized field intensity: 1 at the center, then 0.88 at the fireball radius, 0.58 at severe, 0.34 at moderate, 0.14 at light, and 0 outside. The renderer gives an expanding front a soft tail up to 1.24 times its current radius. At each sampled map point, the domain combination takes the strongest intensity `peak` and the sum `sum`, then returns `clamp(peak + (1 − peak) × (1 − exp(−0.5 × max(0, sum − peak))), 0, 1)`. This response is order-independent and never exceeds 1. More overlapping fields can make the same location brighter without inflating any strike's yield or geographic reach into a virtual larger device.

The multi-field renderer applies a separate **display-only** `intensity^1.85` transfer before the thermal color lookup. A direct live-Esri, pitched five-strike comparison with `Multi-Launch.png` showed that the untransformed field spread a yellow-orange sheet across much of the viewport. The transfer localizes the hottest colors near impacts and makes the red/purple outskirts more translucent. To make partial overlap visibly advance through the legend's color bands, a second rendering-only hue lookup adds `min(0.16, 1.2 × max(0, sum − peak)) × clamp((0.7 − displayIntensity) / 0.3, 0, 1)` to the transferred value, then clamps the result to 1. The accent is full below display intensity 0.4, tapers to zero by 0.7, and therefore does not wash an already hot cluster white. Alpha is looked up from the transferred value **without** this hue accent. Thus a shared purple sample can become pink or red as overlap grows, while the pure overlap result, each strike's geographic radii, yield, timing, and cloud model remain unchanged. These numbers calibrate appearance to the reference; they are not physical laws or damage thresholds.

The map-distance bar is a separate camera reference: meters per CSS pixel at center latitude are approximated by `156543.03392 × max(0.04, cos(latitude)) / 2^zoom`. The renderer chooses a nearby 1/2/5 kilometer label and bounds the drawn width to 54–158 CSS pixels. On a pitched map, scale varies across the screen, so the bar is an illustrative center-scale reference rather than a survey measure at every point. The legend's four bands retain the single-strike descriptions; a tooltip gives the **latest strike's** modeled zone span, while the color field displays bounded combined intensity.

Overlap brightness, palette, shockwave glow, and the scale bar are illustrative presentation choices. The response does not estimate additive pressure, thermal dose, casualties, fallout, or uncertainty. Independent per-click groups and their individual timing are preserved by the application state. A MIRV click places several warheads in one group, each with its own intensity field and impact clock. Multi-session carriers share the first group's general entry bearing, but each clicked geographic target and curved route is separate. Turning the mode off preserves and renders the latest whole group, including its individual missiles and fireballs, while the single ruler describes the latest delivery.

The compositor unprojects one shared viewport grid and keeps only per-sample peak and sum values; it does not retain a distance grid for every strike. The grid uses 560 × 380 samples for fewer than six strikes, 420 × 280 for six to fifteen, and a fixed 320 × 220 for sixteen or more. The fixed dense tier protects small fields from disappearing as strike count grows. Geographic work proceeds in chunks of at most 600,000 sample evaluations and 1,024 candidate strikes per repaint; total work can grow with the number of strikes. A conservative geographic bound skips only effects outside the visible ground. Retained rendering memory and per-repaint work stay bounded, while every application strike remains eligible for later chunks. The cost is softer spatial detail in dense scenes and possible partial field frames after a camera change while remaining contributions finish. No fixed completion time is promised. The WebGL renderer also skips a fireball dome only when its full transformed bound lies outside a viewport clip plane, retaining pitched or edge-crossing domes. None of these drawing limits changes a strike's modeled radius in kilometers.
